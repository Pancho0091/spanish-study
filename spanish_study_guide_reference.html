01

Presente Indicativo A1

The Present Indicative
💬
In plain English
Your default tense for stating facts, describing habits, and expressing who you are. In English: "I work", "she eats", "we live". In Mexican Spanish it also covers confirmed future plans — "Tomorrow I fly to Minneapolis" → Mañana vuelo a Minneapolis (present tense, not future). If you're not sure which tense to use, this one is usually correct.
Conceptual anchor
The tense of objective reality. States facts, habits, and current actions. The anchor of the entire language.
🇲🇽
Presente Pro-Futuro: Mexicans use the present tense for confirmed future plans. "Mañana nos vemos" (Tomorrow we see each other) sounds more natural and certain than the future tense "nos veremos."
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Adds a 5th person: Vosotros (informal "you all") with its own conjugation: habláis, coméis, vivís. Does not exist anywhere in Latin America — Mexico uses ustedes for everyone regardless of formality.
🇦🇷 Argentina Uses Vos instead of tú for the 2nd person singular. Conjugation: drop the 'i' from vosotros form → trabajás, comés, vivís with stress on the final vowel.
🇨🇴 Colombia Ustedeo — uses usted (formal "you") even with close friends, family, and pets. "¿Usted cómo está, mijo?" to your own child is normal in Medellín and Bogotá.
The Presente de Indicativo descends directly from Latin's present indicative — the oldest, most stable tense in all Romance languages. Latin had six grammatical persons; Spanish collapsed "vos" (2nd person plural) into "ustedes" throughout Latin America during colonization, while Spain preserved it as vosotros.

The term Indicative Mood comes from Latin indicare (to point out, to declare). It is the mood of assertion — you are pointing at reality and stating it as fact. This is why it cannot express doubt, hypothesis, or emotion: those require the Subjunctive (the "other mood").
Formation — Regular Verbs
Remove the infinitive ending. Add endings to the stem.

-IR: -o · -es · -e · -imos · -en

The yo form always ends in -o for all regular verbs.

The Present Continuous magnifies what is happening at this literal millisecond. Your profession is not a millisecond activity — it is a stable fact about who you are.
Error Type · Future Tense for Mexican Plans

Using the morphological future ("viajaré") for a confirmed plan sounds stiff and formal in Mexican Spanish — like a news broadcast, not a conversation. The Presente Pro-Futuro ("viajo") is the natural Mexican choice because it drags the future event into the present reality, making it sound guaranteed and immediate.
02

Ser vs. Estar A1 → B1

Both mean "to be" — completely different functions
💬
In plain English
English has one verb — "to be" — for everything. Spanish splits it into two. Ser is for identity and permanent reality: I am a data analyst · she is from Guadalajara · today is Thursday. Estar is for current state: I am tired · the report is ready · he is in a meeting right now. The same adjective changes meaning depending on which you use — es listo = he's clever; está listo = he's ready right now. This distinction doesn't exist in English, which is why it trips everyone up at first.
Conceptual anchor
SER = the blueprint. What something fundamentally IS — identity, essence, origin, objective reality.
ESTAR = the weather. How something IS right now — state, condition, location, temporary reality.

🇲🇽
Mexican culinary critique: "Los tacos son buenos" (objectively good food) vs. "Los tacos están riquísimos" (delicious right now — this specific preparation). Estar = immediate sensory evaluation.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Estar is increasingly used for permanent characteristics in colloquial Peninsular Spanish — especially for appearance. "Estás muy guapa" (you look very beautiful, right now) is standard. In Mexico, this creeps in for compliments too, but formal grammar still prefers ser for character.
🇦🇷 Argentina Argentine Spanish uses estar even more liberally for personality descriptions in casual speech. The ser/estar line is blurrier in Buenos Aires than in Mexico City.
Spanish has two verbs for "to be" because it inherited them from two different Latin verbs with different meanings:

SER comes from Latin esse (to exist, to be at the core). This is the philosophical "being" — what something is at its essence. When you say "soy analista," you're making a statement about your fundamental nature.

ESTAR comes from Latin stare (to stand, to be positioned somewhere). This is the physical "standing" — where something is positioned or how it currently stands. When you say "estoy en la oficina," you're describing a position in space and time.

Most European languages collapsed both into one verb (English: "to be," French: "être"). Spanish kept them separate — giving Spanish speakers a grammatical precision that English simply cannot express in a single word.

Adjectives that change meaning with Ser vs. Estar — Full Context
The same adjective produces two completely different meanings depending on whether it follows ser or estar. This is not a translation error or an exception — it is the entire Ser/Estar system working exactly as designed.

The five-minute test: Ask "can this change in the next five minutes?" If yes → ESTAR (temporary state). If it describes who someone permanently is → SER (identity/character). A clever person is clever tomorrow too → SER. A ready person stops being ready once the meeting starts → ESTAR.

Why "es listo" = clever (not ready):
This is the one that confuses most English speakers. "Listo" with SER describes an innate mental quality — intelligence, sharpness, cleverness — something baked into the person's character. "Listo" with ESTAR describes a state of preparation — ready to go, prepared right now. The English word "ready" only exists as the ESTAR version. The SER version has no clean English equivalent, which is why it feels wrong.

Error Type · Continuous for Professional Identity

This is the most common Continuous error for English speakers, because English uses "I'm working at Adobe" naturally for employment. In Spanish, "estoy trabajando" means you are physically performing work tasks this second — not that Adobe is your employer.

Acentos · SEGA A1

Accent Marks & Syllable Stress — The SEGA System
💬
In plain English
Every Spanish word has one stressed syllable when spoken. The accent mark (tilde — the little dash over a vowel like é, á, ó) is not a decoration. It appears on a word only when the spelling would mislead you about which syllable to stress. If the word stresses the syllable you'd naturally expect based on its ending, there's no mark. If it doesn't, the mark appears exactly on the stressed syllable to correct your prediction. Once you understand the 2-rule prediction system, every single tilde in the language makes sense.
The actual reason a tilde exists on any given word
When a Spanish reader sees a word they've never seen before, they make an automatic prediction about which syllable is stressed — based purely on what the word ends with. That prediction is almost always right.

The tilde exists for the cases where the prediction is wrong. It appears ON the stressed syllable and says: "Ignore what you'd normally predict. Stress here instead."

That's the entire system. No tilde = the reader's prediction is correct. Tilde = the reader's prediction is wrong — here's the real stress.
The reader's prediction — built from the word's last letter
Every Spanish word ends in either a vowel/N/S or something else. That ending generates a prediction:

Word ends in… Reader predicts… Examples where prediction is CORRECT (no tilde)
Vowel, N, or S "Stress the 2nd-to-last syllable" CA-sa · HA-bla · da-TOS → wait, DA-tos · co-MEN
Any other consonant (R, L, D, Z…) "Stress the last syllable" ha-BLAR · ciu-DAD · es-pa-ÑOL

These two predictions are correct for 75–80% of all Spanish words. That's why most words have no tilde — the reader's automatic prediction just works.
When the prediction is WRONG — this is the only time a tilde appears
A tilde appears when a word's actual spoken stress doesn't match what the ending predicts. The tilde is placed exactly on the stressed syllable to say "override the prediction — stress here."

Walkthrough: why does "café" have a tilde?

1 The reader sees cafe (imagining no accent mark for a moment). The word ends in e — a vowel. 2 Vowel ending → prediction: "stress the 2nd-to-last syllable" → the reader would say CA-fe. 3 But the actual spoken word is ca-FÉ. The stress is on the LAST syllable. The prediction was wrong. 4 So a tilde is placed on the vowel of the REAL stressed syllable: café. It says: "ignore the prediction — stress fé, not ca."
Walkthrough: why does "casa" have NO tilde?

1 The word ends in a — a vowel. 2 Vowel ending → prediction: "stress the 2nd-to-last syllable" → the reader predicts CA-sa. 3 The actual spoken word is CA-sa. The prediction is correct. 4 No tilde needed — the reader's automatic prediction already gives the right answer.
Walkthrough: why does "fácil" have a tilde?

1 The word ends in l — a consonant (not N or S). 2 Consonant (not N/S) ending → prediction: "stress the last syllable" → the reader would say fa-CIL. 3 But the actual word is FÁ-cil. Stress is on the FIRST syllable. The prediction was wrong. 4 Tilde on á: fácil. It says: "ignore the prediction — stress fá, not cil."

The one-sentence mental model
"The ending makes a promise. The tilde appears when the word breaks it."

— Vowel/N/S ending promises: "I stress second-to-last."
— Other consonant ending promises: "I stress last."
— Word keeps the promise → no tilde.
— Word breaks the promise → tilde on the REAL stressed syllable.

Pronoun attachment — why commands cascade upward
When you attach object pronouns to a command, the word gets longer but the original stress stays on the same vowel. As the word grows, that vowel migrates further from the end — eventually requiring a tilde to protect it:

prepara → stress on PA (position 2, vowel ending → no mark needed)
prepáralo → same stress on PA, now position 3 → Esdrújula → tilde required
prepáramelo → same stress on PA, now position 4 → Sobreesdrújula → tilde required

The tilde is pinning the stress in place as the word grows. Remove it and a native speaker defaults to the new expected position — completely wrong pronunciation.
Spanish vowels — the other accent rule (always fixed)
A = "ah" · E = "eh" · I = "ee" · O = "oh" · U = "oo"
Unlike English (where "a" in "cat," "cake," "car," "about" produces four different sounds), Spanish vowels are invariant. The same vowel in the same position always sounds identical — stressed or not. This is why syllable stress is so salient in Spanish: everything else stays constant.
🇲🇽
Why this matters for verbs specifically: The preterite tense signal for -AR verbs is entirely carried by the accent mark. habló (he spoke, preterite) vs. habla (he speaks, present) differ only in the ó tilde. Miss it = wrong tense, wrong person, wrong meaning — all from one missing mark.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects — Pronunciation Differences
🇪🇸 Spain Distinción: C before E/I, and Z are pronounced like English "TH" in "thing." gracias = "GRA-thias" · cerveza = "ther-VE-tha." The common myth that this came from a king with a lisp is false — it is simply how medieval Castilian consonants evolved.
🌎 All LatAm Seseo: C, Z, and S all sound identical — all pronounced as "S." cerveza = "ser-VE-sa." This is the universal standard across all 20 Latin American countries.
🇦🇷 Argentina Zheísmo: LL and Y are pronounced like English "SH." "Yo me llamo" = "SHO me SHA-mo." Result of massive Italian immigration in the 19th century reshaping the phonological system.
🇨🇺 Caribbean S-Aspiration: The letter S at the end of syllables is breathed out like "H" or dropped entirely. "Los pasteles" = "Loh pasteleh." Plural markers and verb endings disappear from audio — context carries the grammar.
The Spanish tilde system was codified in the 18th century by the Real Academia Española to resolve print ambiguity — without tildes, readers had no signal for stress, and stress changes meaning entirely (hablo vs. habló).

The deeper logic: Spanish inherited Latin's phonemic stress (stress that changes word identity), but collapsed Latin's distinct verb endings into similar-looking forms. The tilde is a compensatory device — it rescues the distinctions Latin made through sound that Spanish's simplified endings can no longer make automatically.

The Grave category dominates: roughly 75–80% of Spanish words are Grave (stress on second-to-last). This is why the default assumption works so well — most words you encounter need no tilde at all.
Common errors applying SEGA
Error · Over-accenting default Grave words
Los dátos de Snowflake están lístos para el análisis. → Los datos de Snowflake están listos para el análisis.
"Datos" ends in S → default stress is position 2 → DA-tos is already correct → no tilde. "Listos" ends in S → same logic → LIS-tos → no tilde.
Error · Missing tilde on a deviation word
El examen fue facil pero util para el analisis del proyecto. → El examen fue fácil pero útil para el análisis del proyecto.
Three misses, same rule: "fácil" ends in L (consonant, not N/S) → default is stress on position 1 → but stress is on position 2 (FÁ-cil) → deviation → tilde required. "útil" same pattern: ends in L → default position 1 → stress is on position 2 (Ú-til) → tilde required.
Error · Missing verb tense accent (the most costly error)
Mi jefe hablo con el cliente ayer. → Mi jefe habló con el cliente ayer.
"habló" ends in a vowel (o) → default stress is position 2. But the preterite él/ella form stresses the final syllable (ha-BLÓ, position 1) → that deviates from the default → tilde required.

Acentos como Señales de Tiempo A1

Accent Marks as Tense Signals — The Most Important Orthographic Rule
💬
In plain English
One accent mark can flip a verb's entire meaning. habla = he speaks (right now, present tense). habló = he spoke (past tense, done). The only difference is one tilde on the final vowel. Miss it in writing and you've stated the wrong tense and the wrong person simultaneously. This matters especially in Mexican Spanish, which uses the completed past constantly — almost every sentence about something that happened requires one of these accent marks.
Conceptual anchor
A single accent mark on the final vowel of a verb is not a spelling detail — it is a tense switch. Removing or adding one accent mark changes the person, the tense, and sometimes the mood of a verb entirely. This is the most consequential orthographic rule in Spanish.
🇲🇽
Critical for Mexican Spanish: Because Mexican Spanish uses the Preterite constantly (the door slams shut the moment an action is complete), you write preterite verb forms in nearly every sentence about the past. Every time you forget a preterite accent on an -AR verb, you accidentally write present tense — completely changing what you said.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain The tense-distinguishing accent rule is identical across all Spanish dialects — no regional variation. Whether you write in Madrid, Mexico City, or Buenos Aires, habló is always preterite and habla is always present. This is one of the most consistent rules in the entire language.
📝 Formal vs. Digital In informal digital communication (WhatsApp, Instagram), native speakers frequently drop accent marks — "hablo" might appear where "habló" was intended. Context usually resolves ambiguity. In any formal, academic, or exam context, missing accents are marked as errors. On the B1 exam, they cost points.
This phenomenon exists because Latin's verb endings were phonetically distinct — different vowel lengths and stress patterns marked tense automatically. As Latin evolved into Spanish, many of these distinctions collapsed into similar-looking forms. The written accent mark was introduced by the Real Academia Española (18th century) precisely to rescue these collisions — to mark the cases where two valid words would otherwise be written identically. The accent mark is the written record of a spoken stress difference that native speakers produce automatically but learners must consciously mark in writing.
The Core Rule — Where Accents Change Tense
This applies primarily to -AR verbs in the yo and él/ella preterite forms.

-AR yo preterite: ends in -é (stressed) → hablé, trabajé, llegué, busqué
Without accent (-e) = subjunctive / command form: hable, trabaje, llegue, busque

-AR él/ella preterite: ends in -ó (stressed) → habló, trabajó, llegó, buscó
Without accent (-o) = yo present tense: hablo, trabajo, llego, busco

-ER/-IR verbs: The accent is built into the ending itself — it cannot be removed without destroying the form entirely. comí, comió, viví, vivió — these endings always carry their accent marks as part of the standard ending pattern.
The Complete Collision Map — Every Dangerous Pair
-AR verbs — the critical pairs:

hablo = I speak (present)  vs.  habló = he/she spoke (preterite)
trabajo = I work (present)  vs.  trabajó = he/she worked (preterite)
analizo = I analyze (present)  vs.  analizó = he/she analyzed (preterite)
preparo = I prepare (present)  vs.  preparó = he/she prepared (preterite)
horneo = I bake (present)  vs.  horneó = he/she baked (preterite)
llego = I arrive (present)  vs.  llegó = he/she arrived (preterite)
corro = I run (present)  vs.  corrió = he/she ran (preterite — -ER, so -ió)
corre = he/she runs (present)  vs.  corrió = he/she ran (preterite)

-AR yo preterite vs. subjunctive:

hablé = I spoke (preterite)  vs.  hable = speak! / that he speak (subjunctive/command)
trabajé = I worked (preterite)  vs.  trabaje = that I/he work (subjunctive)
llegué = I arrived (preterite)  vs.  llegue = that I/he arrive (subjunctive)
busqué = I looked for (preterite)  vs.  busque = that I/he look for (subjunctive)
empecé = I started (preterite)  vs.  empiece = that I/he start (subjunctive — stem change)

The él pronoun itself:

el = the (definite article)  vs.  él = he (subject pronoun)
"El habló" is wrong on two levels — missing accent on both words.
Why -ER and -IR Preterites Are Self-Protected
-ER and -IR preterite endings carry their accent marks as part of the standard pattern — you cannot write them without the accent because the accent IS the ending:

yo: comí · viví · escribí · salí · dormí ← the í is the entire ending
él/ella: comió · vivió · escribió · salió · durmió ← the ió ending always has accent

However, a related danger: the nosotros form is identical in present and preterite for -AR verbs:
hablamos = we speak (present) AND we spoke (preterite) — no accent, both tenses
Context and time markers ("ayer hablamos" vs. "normalmente hablamos") resolve the ambiguity.
-AR Verb · trabajar / analizar / preparar
-AR · Present vs. Preterite Contrast
Normalmente yo trabajo desde casa los viernes, pero ayer trabajé en la oficina de Adobe.
Normally I work from home on Fridays, but yesterday I worked in the Adobe office. — trabajo (no accent = yo present). trabajé (accent on é = yo preterite). Both in one sentence — the contrast is exact.
-AR · Third Person Contrast
Mi jefe normalmente analiza los datos a las nueve, pero ayer analizó todo por la tarde.
My boss normally analyzes the data at nine, but yesterday he analyzed everything in the afternoon. — analiza (no accent = él/ella present). analizó (accent on ó = él/ella preterite). One accent changes both person-reference and time.
-AR · yo Preterite vs. Subjunctive
Yo llegué a tiempo a la reunión. / El director quiere que yo llegue antes de las nueve.
I arrived on time to the meeting. / The director wants me to arrive before nine. — llegué (accent = yo preterite, completed fact). llegue (no accent = yo subjunctive, desired action). Same pronunciation region, completely different grammar.
-ER Verb · comer / hacer / corer
-ER · Built-in Accent
Felix come comida suave todos los días. Ayer comió su primera comida normal después de la cirugía.
Felix eats soft food every day. Yesterday he ate his first normal meal after surgery. — come (no accent = él/ella present). comió (accent built into -ió ending = él/ella preterite). The -ió ending cannot exist without its accent.
-ER · yo Preterite Self-Accenting
Ayer corrí diez kilómetros por West Adams con mis zapatos Vivobarefoot.
Yesterday I ran ten kilometers through West Adams in my Vivobarefoot shoes. — corrí: the í IS the accent and the ending simultaneously. It is physically impossible to write this without the accent — "corri" would be pronounced differently and is not a valid word.
-IR Verb · vivir / salir / escribir
-IR · Three-Way Contrast
Yo vivo en The Jayne. (present) / Antes vivía cerca de Fig. (imperfect) / El año pasado viví en otro barrio. (preterite)
Three tenses, three forms of vivir — each visually distinct. vivo (present, no accent). vivía (imperfect, accent on the í of -ía). viví (preterite, accent on final í). The accent marks make these immediately distinguishable in writing.
-IR · Narrative in Action
Ayer salí de la oficina a las seis, llegué a The Jayne, abrí Snowflake y escribí el reporte final.
Yesterday I left the office at six, arrived at The Jayne, opened Snowflake and wrote the final report. — Four consecutive preterite verbs driving the narrative forward. Every single one carries an accent mark on the final vowel. Remove any one accent and that verb shifts to a different tense or mood.
Common Errors — High Consequence
Error Type · Missing él/ella Preterite Accent
Mi jefe hablo con el cliente ayer por la tarde. → Mi jefe habló con el cliente ayer por la tarde.
Without the accent, "hablo" = I speak (yo, present tense). The sentence now reads: "My boss I-speak with the client yesterday" — grammatically broken because the subject is "mi jefe" (third person) but the verb form is first person present.
Error Type · Preterite yo vs. Subjunctive Confusion
Ayer trabaje en la oficina de Adobe. → Ayer trabajé en la oficina de Adobe.
"Trabaje" (no accent) = subjunctive form — "that I/he work." "Ayer trabaje" means "Yesterday that I work" — grammatically broken. The accent on trabajé marks it as completed past fact.
Error Type · Nosotros Ambiguity — Use Time Markers
Hablamos con el cliente. — This is ambiguous without context. → Ayer hablamos con el cliente. (preterite) OR Normalmente hablamos con el cliente los viernes. (present)
The nosotros form of -AR verbs is identical in present and preterite — "hablamos" means both "we speak" and "we spoke." Unlike the yo and él/ella forms, there is NO accent to distinguish them. Always use a time marker (ayer, normalmente, todos los días, el lunes pasado) to make the tense unambiguous in writing.
Error Type · Missing él pronoun Accent
El habló con mi jefe ayer. → Él habló con mi jefe ayer.
"El" (no accent) = the definite article "the." "Él" (accent) = the subject pronoun "he." "El habló" = "The spoke" — nonsensical. Both the pronoun and the verb require their accent marks.

Hay, Había, Habrá A1 → A2

Impersonal Haber — There is / There are
💬
In plain English
"There is" and "there are" in English. The key: Spanish uses only one form regardless of quantity — hay un problema (there is one problem) and hay tres problemas (there are three problems) both use hay. Then: había = there was/were (imperfect past), hubo = there was/were (completed past event), habrá = there will be. These three forms cover the vast majority of situations you'll encounter.
Hay · Había · Habrá — one form, three tenses, always singular
hay=present→Hay una reunión. — There is a meeting.
había=past→Había un error. — There was an error.
habrá=future→Habrá cambios. — There will be changes.
Never plural: always había / habrá regardless of how many things
Conceptual anchor
Impersonal HABER announces the existence of something. It is always singular regardless of what follows — no agreement with the noun. One of the most-used structures in all of Spanish.
🇲🇽
Extremely common in Mexican daily speech: "Hay tráfico" (there's traffic), "No hay tiempo" (there's no time), "¿Hay reunión hoy?" (is there a meeting today?) — used constantly at Adobe and in LA.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain In colloquial Peninsular Spanish — especially in southern Spain and among younger speakers — you increasingly hear "habemos" as a plural for haber ("there are we"). This is considered non-standard and is stigmatized in formal writing, but it's spreading. Standard written Spanish always uses hay for singular and plural alike.
🌎 General LatAm In informal spoken Latin American Spanish, "haiga" (non-standard subjunctive of haber) appears in rural and popular speech instead of the correct haya. Never use it in writing or formal contexts.
Hay is a fossilized form — it froze in time from Old Spanish ha y ("there has it" = "it has there"), where y was an archaic adverb meaning "there" (from Latin ibi). Over centuries, the two words fused into a single invariable word.

This explains why hay never changes for number. It is not truly conjugated — it is a lexicalized phrase that became a single grammatical unit. The same Latin habere (to have) that gave us haber as an auxiliary verb also gave us this existence marker — two completely different grammatical functions from one Latin ancestor.
The three core forms — always singular, never changes for number
hay → present: there is / there are
había → imperfect past: there was / there were (ongoing, background)
hubo → preterite: there was (completed, single event)
habrá → future: there will be
habría → conditional: there would be

Critical rule: Never use están or son for existence. Only HABER.
-AR Verb context · trabajar / haber
-AR · Hay (Present Existence)
Hay cinco reuniones en el calendario de Adobe hoy — entre ellas, una revisión de XGBoost y una llamada con el cliente internacional.
There are five meetings in the Adobe calendar today — including an XGBoost review and a call with the international client.
Hay is invariable — it never changes regardless of whether the noun that follows is singular or plural, masculine or feminine. "Hay una reunión" AND "hay cinco reuniones" both use exactly the same form.
-ER Verb context · haber / comer
-IR Verb context · haber / vivir
-IR · Hubo vs. Había Contrast
Había mucho tráfico en la 10 todos los días, y ayer hubo un accidente en la salida de Crenshaw que lo empeoró todo.
There was always a lot of traffic on the 10 every day, and yesterday there was an accident at the Crenshaw exit that made everything worse.
Both forms in one sentence to show the contrast directly. "Había mucho tráfico todos los días" — ongoing, habitual background state = Imperfect.
Common Errors
Error Type · Conjugating Hay for Number
Han muchos errores en el código de Python. → Hay muchos errores en el código de Python.
"Han" is the third-person plural of haber used in the Present Perfect ("han comido" = they have eaten). It is not the plural of hay.
Error Type · Estar for Existence
Están muchos archivos en el servidor de Adobe. → Hay muchos archivos en el servidor de Adobe.
Estar announces location of already-known, specific things. Hay announces existence — introducing something to the conversation for the first time.

🧩

Presente Continuo A2

The Present Continuous
💬
In plain English
The "-ing" tense — but only for right-now, active actions. "I am eating" = estoy comiendo. Important: Spanish uses this much more narrowly than English. You don't use it for jobs ("I am working at Adobe" → wrong; use present simple), habits, or plans. Only for something actively unfolding at this exact moment. Formed with estar + gerund (-ando/-iendo).
Presente Continuo formula
subject+estar (conjugated)+gerundio
-AR→-ando  trabajando · analizando · enviando
-ER / -IR→-iendo  comiendo · viviendo · haciendo
Conceptual anchor
The magnifying glass. Only use it when you want to hyper-focus on an action happening at this exact, literal millisecond — not for general habits or routines.
🇲🇽
"Ahorita" in action: The Present Continuous pairs with Mexican "ahorita" (right now) to signal total focus and un-interruptibility. "Estoy analizando datos" = I am literally in the middle of this process right now.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Peninsular Spanish uses the Present Continuous more frequently than Mexican Spanish — closer to English patterns. Spaniards are more likely to say "estoy trabajando" for a general ongoing situation where a Mexican would simply use "trabajo."
🇦🇷 Argentina Argentine Spanish mirrors Peninsular in using the continuous more broadly. The Italian influence on Argentine Spanish brought a similar continuous structure from Italian stare + gerundio, making it feel more natural in colloquial Rioplatense speech.
The Spanish gerund (-ando/-iendo) descends from the Latin gerundive — a verbal noun form. In classical Latin it expressed obligation ("something to be done"). Over centuries in Vulgar Latin, it shifted function entirely — from obligation to ongoing action description.

Spanish uses estar as the continuous auxiliary (not ser) because stare (to stand, to be positioned) already contained the idea of an ongoing, positioned state. "I am standing in the act of working" → "estoy trabajando." The choice of estar over ser as the continuous helper is not arbitrary — it reflects estar's core DNA of physical, positioned existence.

Futuro Próximo A2

The Near Future — "Going to"
💬
In plain English
"Going to" — exactly like English. ir + a + infinitive: voy a comer = I'm going to eat. This is actually the most natural way to express future plans in Mexican Spanish — more common in speech than the actual "will" future tense. If you want to say something is going to happen, use this. Simple, reliable, sounds fluent immediately.
Futuro Próximo — ir + a + infinitivo
subject+ir (conjugated)+a+infinitivo
Voy a trabajar · Vas a comer · Vamos a terminar el modelo
Conceptual anchor
The engine of intent. IR + a + infinitive. Represents an intention already formed or an event about to happen. Implies physical movement toward the goal.
🇲🇽
The true Mexican future: Mexicans almost never use the formal Simple Future for actual plans. Whether it's happening in 5 minutes or 5 years, if it's a concrete plan, use voy a + infinitive.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Peninsular Spanish uses both the Near Future (voy a + inf.) and the Simple Future (iré) for actual plans — they are largely interchangeable in Spain. A Spaniard might say "mañana iré al médico" and "mañana voy a ir al médico" with equal naturalness.
🌎 Mexico / LatAm In Mexico, the Simple Future (iré) for actual plans sounds stiff and bureaucratic — like reading a legal document. The Near Future (voy a ir) is the only natural choice for real plans at any time horizon.
Periphrasis is the use of multiple words to express what could be a single inflected form. "Voy a comer" (I'm going to eat) is periphrastic — three words doing the job of one: comeré.

This is not laziness — it is a universal tendency in language evolution. English went through the same shift: Old English had a morphological future; Modern English replaced it almost entirely with "going to" and "will." Spanish is in the middle of the same process. The Near Future is winning in Mexico because it encodes intention and present evidence — "I am currently moving toward eating" — giving it communicative precision the single-word form cannot match.

Imperativo Afirmativo A2

The Affirmative Imperative — Commands
💬
In plain English
How to give a direct command: "Eat this", "Come here", "Send it to me". The tú (informal) command uses the él/ella present-tense form — so habla, come, escribe. Usted (formal) commands use the subjunctive. The key quirk: when you attach pronouns, they glue onto the END of the verb — mándamelo (send it to me), not "me lo manda". Add enough pronouns and you'll need an accent mark to keep the original stress in place.
Conceptual anchor
Telling someone to do something. The informal tú command uses the 3rd-person present indicative form. Formal commands (usted) borrow from the subjunctive.
🇲🇽
Enclisis — pronouns attach to the back: Affirmative commands force pronouns to attach to the end of the verb, often requiring an accent mark to protect the original stress. "Envíamelo" = Envía + me + lo.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Spain has a Vosotros imperative that does not exist in Latin America: drop the final -r from the infinitive and add -d. hablar → hablad · comer → comed · venir → venid. In LatAm, the Ustedes form covers all plural commands.

The Affirmative Imperative is one of the oldest grammatical structures in any language — it predates complex syntax. In Latin, the imperative was formed simply by using the bare verb stem without any personal ending: ama! (love!), veni! (come!).

The Spanish informal tú command preserved this: habla, come, vive — it uses the 3rd person singular present (which is itself very close to the bare stem). The formal Usted command borrows from the Subjunctive because commanding a social superior is — grammatically and culturally — entering irrealis territory: you're not asserting reality, you're requesting a change to it.

💬
In plain English

Verbos tipo Gustar A2

Gustar-type Verbs — Reverse-subject verbs
💬
In plain English
Gustar and verbs like it work backwards from English. In English: "I like coffee." In Spanish, it literally says: "Coffee is pleasing to me" → Me gusta el café. The coffee is the subject; you are the indirect object. The verb agrees with what's liked, not with you. So: me gusta (one thing) vs. me gustan (multiple things). This pattern applies to a whole family: encantar (to love), molestar (to bother), parecer (to seem), interesar (to interest), doler (to hurt).
Conceptual anchor
These verbs work backwards from English. In "I like coffee," the subject is I. In Spanish "me gusta el café," the subject is el café — the thing doing the pleasing. The person is the indirect object receiver, not the subject.
🇲🇽
Essential for polite professional Spanish: "Me gustaría" (conditional of gustar) is the most common polite request form in Mexican workplaces. Beyond gustar: interesar, molestar, encantar, parecer, importar, faltar all follow the same reverse logic.

🌎 Mexico Mexican Spanish prefers encantar for stronger positive reactions — "Me encanta" (I love it) — where Spain might use "me chifla" or "me flipa." All follow the same reverse-subject structure.
Gustar comes from Latin gustare — meaning to taste, to sample. In classical Latin it was a transitive verb with a normal subject-object structure: "I taste the wine." Over time in Vulgar Latin, it shifted to describe the experience being felt by a person, inverting the subject-object relationship.

This structural inversion — where the thing doing the pleasing is the grammatical subject — is called a psych verb construction. The "experiencer" (the person feeling the emotion) is demoted to indirect object. Spanish has dozens of these: interesar, molestar, fascinar, aburrir, sorprender. The logic: emotions happen to you, not by you.

Verbos Reflexivos A2

Reflexive Verbs — When the subject does the action to itself
🪞
In plain English
A reflexive verb is one where the subject and object are the same person — the person does the action to themselves. Me levanto = I get (myself) up. But Spanish reflexive form also encodes other meanings beyond literal self-action: daily routines, internal emotional changes, and idiomatic meaning shifts where the verb changes meaning entirely (ir = to go, irse = to leave). The reflexive pronoun (me, te, se, nos, os, se) must come directly before the conjugated verb or attach to non-finite forms.
Three uses of the reflexive form — not just "doing it to yourself"
1. True reflexive: Subject acts on itself → Me ducho (I shower myself)
2. Reciprocal: Each other → Nos llamamos todos los días (We call each other every day)
3. Idiomatic / meaning shift: Verb changes meaning entirely → dormir = to sleep / dormirse = to fall asleep; ir = to go / irse = to leave; poner = to put / ponerse = to put on (clothing) / to become (emotional state)

Without me, the sentence means "I get someone/something up at 7" — as in waking up another person. The reflexive pronoun is never optional in reflexive verbs; it is part of the verb's grammar.

Por vs. Para A2 → B1

Both can mean "for" — completely different directions
💬
In plain English
Both translate as "for" in English, but they point in opposite directions. Por looks backward — it's about cause, reason, exchange, movement through, duration: Gracias por el café · Lo hice por ti · Caminé por el parque · Trabajé por dos horas. Para looks forward — it's about purpose, destination, recipient, deadline: Salgo para Guadalajara · Esto es para ti · Necesito el reporte para el viernes. The shortcut: Por = because of / through / in exchange. Para = in order to / headed toward / due by.
Conceptual anchor
POR = backward-looking. The engine behind — cause, motivation, exchange, movement through space.
PARA = forward-looking (teleological). The target ahead — destination, deadline, recipient, purpose.
🇲🇽
Adobe context: "Trabajo para Adobe" (I work for Adobe — employer) vs. "Trabajo por Adobe" (I work on behalf of / because of Adobe). Para = direction of employment. Por = cause or substitution.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Usage of por and para is consistent across all dialects — this is one of the few areas where Spain and Latin America behave identically. No meaningful regional variation exists in the core grammar. Minor lexical collocations differ (certain fixed phrases prefer one over the other regionally) but the fundamental rules are universal.
⚠️ Common Error English speakers confuse por/para because both can translate to "for." The fix: para = forward arrow (destination, recipient, deadline, goal). por = backward arrow (cause, motivation, exchange, route traversed).
The confusion between por and para exists entirely because English collapsed two Latin prepositions into one word:

Latin PER → Spanish por — expressed movement through, agency, cause: "per viam" (through the road), "per te" (because of you/through you)
Latin PRO → Spanish para — expressed forward motion toward a goal, benefit for someone: "pro patria" (for the homeland — on its behalf/toward its benefit)

English took both and merged them into "for." Spanish kept them separate, preserving a grammatical precision that encodes the direction of causality — backward-looking cause (por) vs. forward-looking purpose (para).

Infinitivo vs. Que + Subjuntivo A2 → B1

When to use infinitive vs. when to trigger subjunctive
💬
In plain English
When two verbs connect in a sentence, Spanish chooses between two structures. Same subject (I want to eat) → use the infinitive directly: Quiero comer. Different subjects (I want you to eat) → use que + subjunctive: Quiero que comas. This is the gateway to the subjunctive. The rule is dead simple once you see it: one person = infinitive, two people = que + subjunctive. The challenge is recognizing which verbs trigger the subjunctive (want, need, hope, prefer, order, recommend).
Conceptual anchor
One subject = infinitive. Two subjects = subjunctive with que. This single rule governs hundreds of sentences. If the same person wants something and does it — infinitive. If one person wants another person to do it — que + subjunctive.
🇲🇽
The Adobe daily split: "Quiero terminar el análisis" (I want to finish — same person) vs. "Quiero que tú termines el análisis" (I want you to finish — different person, subjunctive triggered).
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Spain occasionally allows infinitive with different subjects in informal speech: "Quiero tú hacerlo" instead of "Quiero que tú lo hagas." This is considered non-standard even in Spain but appears in casual Peninsular conversation. Never use it in writing.
🌎 All dialects The single-subject infinitive rule is universal. "Quiero comer" (same subject = infinitive) vs. "Quiero que comas" (different subject = subjunctive) is consistent from Madrid to Buenos Aires to Mexico City.
The word que (that) in "quiero que comas" is a complementizer — a word that introduces a subordinate clause and links it to the main clause. It comes from Latin quod (that, which).

When there is a change of subject, Spanish requires the full subordinate clause with que + subjunctive because the second action is now in someone else's control. You cannot encode someone else's action as part of your own infinitive — that would grammatically imply you are doing the action yourself.

This is the same logic English uses: "I want to eat" (me) vs. "I want you to eat" (you). English uses the accusative + infinitive construction. Spanish uses the complementizer + subjunctive. Both languages handle the same conceptual problem of cross-subject control — just differently.
The decision rule
Same subject → infinitive (no que):
Yo quiero comer.  ·  Necesito terminar el reporte.  ·  Prefiero trabajar desde casa.

Different subject → que + subjunctive:
Quiero que tú comas.  ·  Necesito que el equipo termine.  ·  Prefiero que vengas tú.

Verbs that trigger this split: querer · necesitar · preferir · pedir · recomendar · sugerir · permitir · prohibir
-AR Verb · terminar / analizar
-AR · Same Subject = Infinitive, No "que"
Necesito terminar el análisis de XGBoost antes del viernes — lo haré yo mismo esta noche en Snowflake.

Same subject throughout: I need, I finish, I'll do. When the same person is both the wanter and the doer, you use the infinitive directly after the trigger verb — no "que," no subjunctive, no subordinate clause.
-ER Verb · hacer / comer

Common Errors
Error Type · Subjunctive With Same Subject
Quiero que termine el proyecto yo mismo esta semana. → Quiero terminar el proyecto yo mismo esta semana.
"Yo mismo" confirms it is the same person wanting and doing. The subjunctive + "que" implies a second subject is being addressed.
Error Type · Indicative After Volition Trigger
Quiero que mi colega revisa el análisis de Snowflake esta tarde. → Quiero que mi colega revise el análisis de Snowflake esta tarde.
"Revisa" is present indicative — it states a fact ("my colleague reviews"). "Quiero que revisa" reads as "I want that my colleague reviews" — asserting the reviewing as a current fact rather than expressing a desire about a future action.

Comparativos y Superlativos A2 → B1

Comparatives & Superlatives
💬
In plain English
"More than", "less than", "as…as", "the most". Structurally similar to English and fairly easy. The pattern: más/menos + adjective + que for comparisons; el/la más + adjective for superlatives. The only real traps are the four irregular forms you'll use constantly: mejor (better/best), peor (worse/worst), mayor (older/greatest), menor (younger/least). These four replace "más bueno", "más malo", "más grande" in most contexts.
Conceptual anchor
Three comparison structures: more than (superiority), less than (inferiority), and as...as (equality). Superlatives add "the most/least" — the extreme end of the scale.
🇲🇽
Appears in every B1 writing task: Comparing data, describing options, writing opinion paragraphs — comparatives are unavoidable. "Este modelo es más preciso que el anterior" is exactly the kind of sentence Adobe analytics produces.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain In colloquial Peninsular Spanish, "más mejor" and "más peor" appear in informal speech (using regular comparative with already-irregular forms). These are stigmatized as uneducated — never use in writing. The correct forms are always mejor and peor alone.
🌎 Mexico / LatAm The same error exists in informal Latin American speech. "Más mejor" is heard but universally recognized as non-standard. Additionally, Mexican Spanish uses "chido/mejor" in informal registers where Spain uses "guay/mejor."
The irregular comparatives mejor, peor, mayor, menor are called suppletive forms — they come from completely different Latin words than their base adjectives:

bueno (Latin bonus) → mejor (Latin melior) — entirely different root
malo (Latin malus) → peor (Latin pejor) — entirely different root

This is why you cannot say "más bueno" for the comparative — Latin already had a dedicated word for "more good" (melior), and Spanish inherited it directly. The same phenomenon occurs in English: good → better (not "more good"), bad → worse (not "more bad"). Both languages inherited their irregulars from the same Latin suppletive system.
The three comparison formulas
Superiority: más + adjective/adverb + que → más rápido que
Inferiority: menos + adjective/adverb + que → menos costoso que
Equality: tan + adjective/adverb + como → tan preciso como
Equality (nouns): tanto/a/os/as + noun + como → tantos datos como

Superlative: el/la/los/las + más/menos + adjective
El análisis más complejo · La solución más eficiente · Los datos más recientes

Irregular comparatives — no más/menos:
bueno → mejor (better) · malo → peor (worse)
grande → mayor (older/greater) · pequeño → menor (younger/lesser)
-AR Verb · trabajar / analizar
-AR · Superiority + Context

Más + adjective + que = superiority. The adjective must agree with the subject: "sabroso" agrees with "espresso" (masculine singular).

-IR · Inferiority + Mayor/Menor Age

Inferiority comparison (menos + adjective + que) and use of mayor. Mayor can mean "greater" (in size, quantity, or importance) or "older" (in age).

Error Type · Double Comparative With Irregular Form

"Mejor" already encodes the comparative — it means "more good / better." Adding "más" doubles the comparison redundantly, like saying "more better" in English. The same applies to all four irregular comparatives: mejor (never "más bueno"), peor (never "más malo"), mayor (never "más grande" for age), menor (never "más pequeño" for age).

El modelo de Python es tan preciso que el de R. → El modelo de Python es tan preciso como el de R.
"Tan...que" introduces a result or consequence clause, not equality: "El modelo es tan preciso que todos lo usan" (The model is so precise that everyone uses it). "Tan...como" is the equality formula: "as...as." Confusing these two creates sentences with completely different meanings.

Pretérito Pluscuamperfecto B1

The Past Perfect — Pluperfect
💬
In plain English
The "had done" tense — for actions that were already finished before another past event. "When I arrived, she had already left." Two past events: the one that happened earlier uses this tense. Formed with había/habías/había… + past participle. The participle never changes form. The most common use: setting up background that explains why a past situation was already a certain way when the main story begins.
Pluscuamperfecto — past before another past
subject+había / habías / había / habíamos / habían+participio (-ado / -ido)
earlier event (pluscuamp.)←—later event (pretérito)
Cuando llegué, ya habían terminado la reunión.
Conceptual anchor
The deep background. "The past of the past." Its entire purpose is to establish that something had already happened before another past event. A relative tense — it cannot stand alone.
🇲🇽
Narrative sophistication: The Pluperfect lets you manipulate time in a story. You can start at the climax and flashback: "When I arrived at the office... my boss had already sent the email." This is B1 storytelling.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain In Peninsular Spanish, the Pluperfect is used identically to Latin American Spanish — this tense has no regional variation in form or function. It is one of the most consistent structures across all dialects. The formation (había + participio) and meaning (past of the past) are universal.
📚 Register note The Pluperfect is inherently a literary and formal tense across all dialects. It elevates register immediately. In casual Mexican conversation, speakers often simplify complex timelines using just preterite and context rather than the Pluperfect — but it's fully understood and used in professional and written contexts.
The Pluperfect is a compound tense — it uses an auxiliary verb (haber) plus a past participle. Latin had a synthetic pluperfect (amaveram — I had loved) that was a single inflected word. As Latin evolved into Spanish, these single-word forms collapsed and were replaced by analytical (compound) structures using haber.

The word Pluperfect comes from Latin plus quam perfectum — "more than perfect/completed." It is the super-past: an action that was already completed before another past action. This layered chronology — past inside past — is what gives sophisticated narrative its depth. Without the Pluperfect, all past events are on the same timeline. With it, you can establish a causal backstory.

Futuro Simple B1

The Simple Future — "Will"
💬
In plain English
The "will" future — but in Mexican Spanish it's not used for plans. Plans and intentions use the near future (voy a comer) or even the present tense. The Simple Future is used for: (1) genuine uncertainty and speculation — ¿Dónde estará? (I wonder where he is), (2) formal/written declarations, and (3) the second half of conditional sentences. Formed by adding endings directly to the full infinitive: hablar → hablaré, hablarás, hablará…

Conceptual anchor
The "Scooby-Doo" tense. In Mexican Spanish this is almost never used for actual plans. Its real function is epistemic modality — expressing probability, wonder, or conjecture about the present moment.
🇲🇽
Wonder, not plans: "¿Quién será?" doesn't mean "Who will it be tomorrow?" — it means "I wonder who it is right now." Use Futuro Próximo for plans. Use Futuro Simple for guessing and wondering.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain In Spain, the Simple Future is used naturally for actual future plans — not just for conjecture. "Mañana iré al médico" (Tomorrow I will go to the doctor) sounds completely normal to a Spaniard. The epistemic/Scooby-Doo use exists, but the future-as-plan use is equally alive.
🌎 Mexico In Mexico, saying "mañana iré al médico" sounds formal, written, or slightly distant — like a news report. Natural speech uses voy a ir al médico. The morphological future's primary living function in Mexican speech is epistemic conjecture: "¿Dónde estará?" (I wonder where he is).
The Spanish Simple Future has one of the most fascinating etymologies in the language. It did not exist in classical Latin — it was invented in Vulgar Latin by fusing the infinitive with the present tense of habere (to have):

viajar + hé → viajaré (I will travel / "I have to travel")
viajar + has → viajarás
viajar + ha → viajará

This is why the future endings always carry accent marks and attach to the full infinitive — they are the old conjugated forms of "to have" fused onto the end. The irregular stems (tener → tendr-, hacer → har-) show where the infinitive was phonetically compressed before the fusion happened. You are literally saying "I have-to-travel" every time you say "viajaré."

The Simple Conditional — "Would"
💬
In plain English
The "would" tense — for hypotheticals, polite requests, and "if…then" consequences. Si tuviera tiempo, viajaría más (If I had time, I would travel more). Also used for softening requests: ¿Podría ayudarme? (Could you help me?) sounds much more polite than ¿Puede ayudarme?. Uses the same stems as the Future tense + endings that look like Imperfecto (-ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -ían). Critical: every single form has an accent on the í — missing them is an instant error.
Condicional = same irregular stems as futuro + -ía endings

Conceptual anchor
The anatomy of "would". Creates a hypothetical scenario — an action that depends on a condition being met. Also the ultimate shield of politeness in daily Mexican speech.
🇲🇽
The Mexican gold standard for politeness: "Me gustaría" (I would like) is infinitely more polite than "Quiero" (I want). The conditional cushions every request, preserving the listener's negative face. Default to it.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain In colloquial Peninsular Spanish, especially in northern Spain (Navarra, País Vasco), the Conditional is sometimes replaced by the Imperfect in "if" clauses: "Si tenía tiempo, iba" instead of the standard "Si tuviera tiempo, iría." 🌎 All dialects The politeness function of the Conditional ("Me gustaría," "¿Podría?") is universal across all Spanish dialects — the most consistent use of this tense worldwide. No regional variation in the polite request structure.

Future: viajar + hé → viajaré (present of haber = certain future)
Conditional: viajar + había → viajaría (imperfect of haber = hypothetical)

The Imperfect of haber encoded a past, unresolved situation — "I used to have to travel." That sense of unrealized, contingent obligation became the grammatical DNA of the Conditional. This is why the Conditional and Future share identical irregular stems — they were built from the same infinitive base, just with different tenses of habere fused onto the end.

Imperativo Negativo B1

The Negative Imperative
💬
In plain English
"Don't do this." Negative commands are structurally the opposite of affirmative commands in two ways. First, they use the subjunctive form instead of the present tense: no hables, no comas, no escribas. Second, pronouns go before the verb (not attached after): no me lo envíes (don't send it to me). If you get these two things right — subjunctive form, pronouns in front — negative commands are locked.
Negative imperative — uses subjuntivo, not indicativo
no+presente subjuntivo→¡No hagas eso! · ¡No vayas! · ¡No lo digas!
affirmative: ¡habla! vs. negative: ¡no hables!
Conceptual anchor
Completely structurally divorced from the affirmative. The moment a command becomes negative, Spanish forces a shift to the subjunctive mood — because telling someone NOT to do something is discussing a hypothetical, non-existent action.
🇲🇽
Proclisis — pronouns float in front: The pronoun rule flips entirely. In negative commands, pronouns cannot attach to the end — they must float between "No" and the verb. "No me lo des" (Don't give it to me).
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain The Negative Imperative structure (No + Subjunctive) is identical across all dialects — no regional variation. Spain additionally has the Vosotros negative: no habléis, no comáis — which Latin America does not use.
🇦🇷 Argentina The Vos negative imperative: no hablés, no comás, no vengás — using the Vos subjunctive form. Different from both the Tú and Usted negative imperatives. The pattern: No + [infinitive minus -r, with accent on last vowel].
The shift from Affirmative to Negative Imperative is not just grammatical housekeeping — it reflects a deep cognitive distinction between Realis and Irrealis moods.

An Affirmative command (habla) describes an action you want to enter reality. It uses the Indicative because you are rooting the command in the real world — "do this real thing."

A Negative command (no hables) describes an action that should not exist — a non-event, a hypothetical non-reality. Grammarians call this the Irrealis mood: you are legislating against a reality that hasn't happened yet. The Subjunctive — the mood of the unreal, the hypothetical, the not-yet-asserted — is the only grammatically appropriate tool for commanding someone to maintain a non-reality.

Usted: No + subjunctive usted form

Irregulars: ir→no vayas · ser→no seas · saber→no sepas · estar→no estés · dar→no des

"No habla" is present indicative third person: "he/she doesn't speak." This is a statement, not a command. To create a negative command, the verb must switch to the subjunctive form entirely — not just add "no" in front of the affirmative.

"No envíamelo" fails on two levels: (1) "envíame" uses the affirmative command form (envía) — but negative commands require the subjunctive (envíes). (2) The pronouns are attached to the back (enclisis) — but negative commands require proclisis (pronouns float before the verb). Both the verb form and the pronoun position must change simultaneously.

Presente de Subjuntivo B1

The Present Subjunctive
💬
In plain English
Not a tense — a mood. The Indicative mood states facts. The Subjunctive mood appears when the main clause expresses something unreal, uncertain, emotional, or desired. It almost always appears in a subordinate clause after que: Quiero que vengas (I want you to come — "come" is in subjunctive because your coming is a desire, not a fact). The trigger verbs are: want, need, hope, prefer, recommend, doubt, be happy that, be necessary that. If the main verb is one of those, the second verb (different subject) goes subjunctive. It's the hardest concept in Spanish — but the logic is consistent.
Build presente subjuntivo in 3 steps from the yo form
Step 1→yo indicativo hablo · como · pongo · tengo
Step 2→drop -o habl- · com- · pong- · teng-
Step 3→opposite vowel endings -AR → -e/es/e/emos/éis/en  |  -ER/-IR → -a/as/a/amos/áis/an
Conceptual anchor
The parallel universe. The Indicative is the realm of objective reality. The Subjunctive governs the unasserted — hopes, fears, doubts, emotions, volition. It almost exclusively lives inside subordinate clauses triggered by que.
🇲🇽
Social lubrication: The subjunctive is the grammatical tool for harmony and politeness in Mexican professional life. "Quiero que revises el modelo" frames your request as a hope, not a demand. Adobe context = use this constantly.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Peninsular Spanish uses the Subjunctive more frequently than Latin American dialects, particularly in indirect speech and reporting clauses. "Dice que sea verdad" (He says it may be true) is more natural in Spain than in Mexico, where "Dice que es verdad" (Indicative) would often replace it in casual speech.
🌎 Mexico / LatAm Mexican Spanish uses the Subjunctive robustly in all standard contexts, but avoids some of the archaic Subjunctive structures that persist in Peninsular formal writing. The focus is on present-day functional uses: wishes, emotions, doubt, and after ojalá.
All human languages distinguish between two fundamental grammatical worlds:

Realis (Latin: res = thing) — the realm of things that are, that exist, that can be pointed to. The Indicative Mood governs this world.

Irrealis (Latin: irrealis = not real) — the realm of things that might be, are hoped for, are feared, are doubted. The Subjunctive Mood governs this world.

When you say "Quiero que vengas," the coming has not happened yet — it exists only as a desire in your mind. The Subjunctive grammatically marks this as not yet real. The moment it happens, you'd switch to Indicative to describe the reality: "Vine" (I came).

This is why the Subjunctive is triggered by verbs of emotion, doubt, and volition — all three categories describe subjective mental states about an unasserted reality, not objective facts.

Conectores del Discurso B1

Discourse Connectors — The glue of B1 writing and speaking
💬
In plain English
The words that link sentences and paragraphs — "however", "therefore", "in addition", "although", "as a result". In English you use these automatically. In Spanish, using them correctly is what separates B1-level output from a series of disconnected sentences. The most important ones to lock immediately: sin embargo (however), además (in addition), aunque (although/even though), por lo tanto (therefore), es decir (that is to say). Master these six and your written Spanish immediately sounds like a fluent speaker.
Conceptual anchor
Connectors are what separate A2 writing (a list of sentences) from B1 writing (a coherent argument). The B1 exam always tests your ability to link ideas logically, contrast them, and build conclusions.
🇲🇽
B1 exam writing tasks require these explicitly: Opinion paragraphs, formal letters, and narratives are all scored on cohesion. Knowing the right connector for each logical relationship is a direct point earner.
🌍 Spain & Other Dialects
🇪🇸 Spain Peninsular Spanish favors certain connectors over others: sin embargo (however) is universal, but Spain more commonly uses no obstante in formal writing where Latin America prefers sin embargo. Spain also uses asimismo (likewise) heavily in formal text — used but less frequent in Mexican professional writing.
🌎 Mexico / LatAm Mexican formal writing favors por lo tanto (therefore) and además (furthermore) as the go-to connectors. The B1 exam expects you to use a variety — don't over-rely on one. The most valued: sin embargo, además, por lo tanto, en conclusión, aunque, por eso.
Discourse connectors belong to a category linguists call cohesive devices — tools that bind sentences into a unified text. Without them, writing is merely a list of observations. With them, writing becomes an argument with internal logic.

Each connector class encodes a specific logical relationship between propositions:
Additive (además, también) — "this is true AND this is also true"
Adversative (sin embargo, aunque) — "this is true BUT this other thing complicates it"
Causal (porque, ya que) — "this is true BECAUSE of this"
Consecutive (por eso, por lo tanto) — "this is true THEREFORE this follows"

The B1 exam is explicitly testing your ability to encode logical relationships in Spanish — not just list facts, but construct arguments. Connectors are the grammar of reasoning.

"Además" adds a second argument that reinforces and strengthens the first — it is an additive connector. Punctuation rule: Además always starts the new clause (after a period or semicolon) and is followed by a comma: "Además, [clause]." Never mid-sentence without punctuation.
-ER Verb · comer / hacer
-IR Verb · vivir / salir

"Porque" introduces the cause (the neighborhood's character motivated the choice to live there). "Por eso" introduces the consequence (the love of the neighborhood leads to the Sunday runs).
Common Errors
Error Type · Sin Embargo Without Punctuation Boundaries
Me gusta Adobe sin embargo el trayecto es muy largo todos los días. → Me gusta Adobe. Sin embargo, el trayecto es muy largo todos los días.
"Sin embargo" is a sentence connector — it must be preceded by a period (or semicolon) and followed by a comma. Using it mid-sentence without any punctuation creates a run-on that is penalized on the B1 writing exam.
Error Type · Porque/Por Eso Directional Confusion
No terminé el análisis de Snowflake porque eso tuve reuniones todo el día. → Tuve reuniones todo el día; por eso no terminé el análisis de Snowflake.
"Porque eso" fuses two separate connectors into a nonsensical hybrid. "Porque" (one word) introduces the cause and looks backward.

La Voz Pasiva B1

The Passive Voice — ser + participio vs. estar + participio
🔄
In plain English
Spanish has two passive-looking structures that mean completely different things. Ser + participio describes an action being done (the door is being locked). Estar + participio describes a resulting state (the door is already locked — the action is done). The difference is event vs. condition. Get this wrong and the sentence says something different from what you mean.
Three passive structures
ACTIONsubject+ser+participio(+por agent)
STATEsubject+estar+participio (agrees masc/fem/sg/pl)
NATURALse+verb 3rd person→ most common in speech
The two passive structures — action vs. state
ser + participio = passive voice (action being performed by an agent) — the event
estar + participio = resultant state (condition resulting from a completed action) — the aftermath
The participio agrees in gender and number with the subject in both cases.
StructureFormulaMeaningExample
Ser pasivaser + participio (+ por + agent)Action performed by someone/somethingEl informe fue escrito por el equipo.
Estar + estadoestar + participioResultant conditionEl informe está escrito — ya está listo.
Se pasivase + verb (3rd person)Passive without agent (very common in Spanish)Se publicaron los resultados ayer.
The se-pasiva — why it dominates spoken Spanish
In everyday Spanish, ser + participio sounds formal. The se passive is far more common when there's no agent:
Formal: El modelo fue desarrollado en Snowflake.
Natural: El modelo se desarrolló en Snowflake.
The se passive agrees with the grammatical subject: Se publicó el artículo / Se publicaron los artículos.
Participio agreement — matches the subject, not the agent
The participio acts like an adjective and must agree with the subject in gender and number:
El análisis fue completado. (masc sing)
La reunión fue cancelada. (fem sing)
Los modelos fueron revisados. (masc pl)
Las tablas fueron actualizadas. (fem pl)
Ser pasiva — the action happened
El dashboard fue desarrollado por mi equipo en Adobe durante el Q3.
The dashboard was developed by my team at Adobe during Q3.
fue (ser, preterite, 3rd sg) + desarrollado (participio, masc sing to agree with el dashboard) + por mi equipo (agent). Classic ser-pasiva structure.
Estar + participio — the resulting state
No te preocupes, el informe ya está escrito y las tablas ya están actualizadas.
Don't worry, the report is already written and the tables are already updated.
está escrito = not "is being written" but "is (in the state of being) written" — completed, done. están actualizadas = the tables are in a state of having been updated. Both describe conditions, not actions. escrito (masc sg), actualizadas (fem pl) — both agree with their subjects.
Ser vs. Estar confusion in passive
La reunión es cancelada. → La reunión fue cancelada. / La reunión está cancelada. / La reunión se canceló.
es cancelada in present tense ser-pasiva sounds unnatural for a one-time event. For completed event: fue cancelada (it was canceled).

El Estilo Indirecto B1

Indirect Speech — Reporting what someone said
💬
In plain English
Indirect speech is how you report what someone said without quoting them directly. "He said: 'I am tired'" becomes "He said that he was tired." In Spanish, when the reporting verb is in the past (dijo, explicó, comentó), the tense of the reported clause shifts back one level. Present becomes imperfect. Preterite becomes pluperfect. This is called "sequence of tenses" — Spanish enforces it more consistently than English does.
Tense backshift — only when reporting verb is past (dijo / explicó)
Presente →Imperfecto·dijo que estaba cansado
Futuro →Condicional·dijo que llegaría tarde
Pretérito →Pluscuamperfecto·dijo que había terminado
Imperativo →Imp. Subjuntivo·me dijo que lo enviara
dice que → NO SHIFT·dice que está cansado
The tense-backshift rule — when reporting verb is past
When dijo / explicó / comentó (past) introduces the report, the verb inside the que clause shifts back one tense level. When reporting verb is present (dice), no shift occurs.
Direct speech tenseIndirect speech tense (after past verb)Example
Presente → "Estoy cansado"Imperfecto → dijo que estaba cansadoHe said he was tired
Futuro → "Llegaré tarde"Condicional → dijo que llegaría tardeHe said he would arrive late
Pretérito → "Terminé el informe"Pluscuamperfecto → dijo que había terminado el informeHe said he had finished the report
Imperativo → "¡Envíalo!"Imperfecto subj. → me dijo que lo enviaraHe told me to send it
Presente (no shift) → "dice que…"No change — dice que está cansadoHe says he is tired
Pronoun and time-expression shifts in indirect speech
Pronouns and time words must shift perspective when reporting:
"Yo trabajaré mañana" → Dijo que él trabajaría al día siguiente
"Ven aquí" → Me dijo que fuera allí
Time words: hoy → ese día; ayer → el día anterior; mañana → al día siguiente; aquí → allí
Reporting a colleague's statement
Mi jefe dijo que el modelo necesitaba más datos de entrenamiento y que lo revisaríamos juntos el viernes.
My boss said that the model needed more training data and that we would review it together on Friday.
dijo (past reporting verb) triggers backshift: necesita → necesitaba (pres → imp), revisaremos → revisaríamos (fut → cond). Both shifts mandatory because the reporting verb is in the past.
No tense shift after present reporting verb
Mi jefa dice que el análisis necesitara revisión. → Mi jefa dice que el análisis necesita revisión.
When the reporting verb is present (dice), no tense shift occurs. The reported clause stays in the same tense as the original statement.

Frases Condicionales con Si B1

Conditional Si-Clauses — Real, likely, and hypothetical conditions
⚖️
In plain English
Spanish if-clauses follow strict tense pairing rules depending on how real the condition is. Real/likely condition: Si + present → future. Hypothetical/unlikely: Si + imperfect subjunctive → conditional. The B1 exam tests both. The subjunctive form after si is always imperfect subjunctive (never present subjunctive — this is the rule that catches everyone).
Si-clause tense pairing
Real / possiblesi+presente→futuro / presente
Hypotheticalsi+imp. subjuntivo→condicional
Past impossiblesi+pluscuamp. subj.→cond. compuesto
❌ NEVER: si + presente subjuntivo
Condition typeSi-clauseResult clauseExample
Real / possible (likely to happen)si + presente indicativofuturo simple / presenteSi tengo tiempo, lo terminaré.
Hypothetical (unlikely / contrary to fact)si + imperfecto subjuntivocondicional simpleSi tuviera más datos, construiría el modelo.
Past hypothetical / impossiblesi + pluscuamperfecto subjuntivocondicional compuestoSi hubiera sabido, habría avisado.
The critical rule — NEVER use present subjunctive after si
❌ Si tenga tiempo… — Wrong. Present subjunctive never follows si in conditional sentences.
✅ Si tengo tiempo… — Real condition (present indicative)
✅ Si tuviera tiempo… — Hypothetical (imperfect subjunctive)

This is the single most common error at B1. The subjunctive IS used in other parts of sentences with si (in other senses), but never in conditional si-clauses.
Imperfect subjunctive — both forms are valid
Spanish has two imperfect subjunctive endings — both are correct, -ra is more common:
-ra form: tuviera, fuera, hiciera, pudiera, dijera
-se form: tuviese, fuese, hiciese, pudiese, dijese
In Latin America, -ra dominates. In Spain, -se appears more in formal writing.
Real condition — present → future
Si terminamos el análisis de Snowflake esta semana, podremos presentarlo el jueves.
If we finish the Snowflake analysis this week, we'll be able to present it on Thursday.
Real condition — we might actually finish it. si + presente indicativo (terminamos) + futuro (podremos). The condition is genuinely possible.
Hypothetical — imperfect subjunctive → conditional
Si tuviera acceso a los datos históricos completos, construiría un modelo predictivo mucho más preciso.
If I had access to the complete historical data, I would build a much more accurate predictive model.
Hypothetical — I don't currently have access. si + imperfecto subjuntivo (tuviera) + condicional (construiría). The gap between this and the real-condition example: the first is "we might," the second is "I don't but imagine if." Both are B1 exam material.

Estar + Participio B1

Resultant State — The condition after an action is complete
✅
In plain English
Estar + participio describes a state that exists because something already happened. The door isn't "being closed" — it's "closed" (in a state of closedness). In English we often use the same word for both, which is why this confuses learners. The key question: is this an ongoing action (ser + participio) or a resulting condition (estar + participio)? If the action is done and what remains is the result, use estar.
estar + participio — resultant state formula
subject+estar+participio (agrees: masc/fem · sg/pl)
masc sg→El informe está terminado.
fem sg→La reunión está cancelada.
masc pl→Los modelos están validados.
fem pl→Las tablas están actualizadas.
The formula and agreement rule
estar + participio — the participio functions as an adjective and must agree with the subject in gender and number:
La ventana está cerrada. (fem sg) | Los archivos están guardados. (masc pl)
Condition typeExampleWhat it means
Physical state of objectLa puerta está abierta.The door is open (result of someone opening it)
Completed work productEl informe está terminado.The report is done (finished — the work is complete)
System/tool stateEl modelo está entrenado.The model is trained (training process complete)
Person's emotional/physical stateEstoy agotado.I'm exhausted (result of exertion)
Relationship statusEstán casados.They are married (result of having married)
Ser vs. Estar + participio — the action/state distinction
La ventana fue cerrada por el empleado. → ser-pasiva: an employee performed the action of closing it
La ventana está cerrada. → estar-estado: the window is currently in the closed state

Test: Can you add por [agent]? → Use ser. Is it just describing a current condition? → Use estar.
Work-completion context
Tranquilo, todos los modelos ya están validados y las queries están optimizadas para el lanzamiento.
Relax, all the models are already validated and the queries are optimized for the launch.
están validados (masc pl — los modelos) — the validation process is complete; what remains is the validated state. están optimizadas (fem pl — las queries) — optimization is done, the resulting state is optimized. Both estar + participio describing the aftermath of completed work.
Using ser when estar is needed
La reunión es cancelada. → La reunión está cancelada. (state) / La reunión fue cancelada. (event)
es cancelada in present tense implies the meeting is perpetually in the act of being canceled — which makes no sense. está cancelada = it's currently in a canceled state (the current condition). fue cancelada = the cancellation event happened in the past.
