Parts of Speech A1

The Seven Building Blocks — Know the jobs before applying the rules
After this section you can
  • Identify the grammatical role (noun, verb, adjective, etc.) of any word in a sentence
  • Understand why Spanish rules work: adjectives match nouns, pronouns replace nouns, adverbs never change form
  • Read any grammar rule and know which word type it applies to before memorizing any endings
Every Spanish grammar rule you will learn is really just a rule about how one type of word interacts with another type. Before memorizing any endings, know what job each word is doing.
Identify the grammatical role (noun, verb, adjective, etc.) of any word in a sentence.
1
Noun
The "Thing"
A person, place, thing, or idea. If you can point to it, touch it, or name a concept, it's a noun.
coffee, house, dog, happiness, Maria.
Spanish: these words have gender → el or la
2
Verb
The "Action"
The engine of the sentence. It tells you what is happening or what state someone is in.
run, drink, sleep, is, are.
Spanish: infinitives end in -ar, -er, or -ir before you conjugate them
3
Adjective
The "Decorator"
Its only job is to describe a noun. Tells you what kind, which one, or how many.
tall, red, spicy, loud, difficult.
Spanish: adjectives must match the gender of the noun they describe
4
Adverb
The "How It Happened" Word
Describes an action (verb), not a thing (noun). Tells you how, when, where, or to what extent. In English, often ends in "-ly."
quickly, very, yesterday, always, well.
Spanish: adverbs never change their form — no gender, no number
5
Pronoun
The "Stand-In"
Replaces a noun so you don't have to keep repeating the same word.
he, she, it, they, us, him.
Spanish: this is where lo, la, and le come in — "I drink it" instead of "I drink the espresso"
6
Article
The "Announcer"
Sits in front of a noun to introduce it. Signals whether you mean a specific thing or a general one.
the, a, an.
Spanish: el, la, los, las (the) and un, una (a/an) — and they must match noun gender
7
Preposition
The "Position" Word
Shows the relationship between things — usually involving time, direction, or location. Classic test: anything a mouse can do to a box (in, on, under, around, through).
in, at, on, with, for, to.
Spanish: por and para both mean "for" — but do completely different jobs
All 7 roles in one sentence
The hungry cat quickly ate it.
Article → introduces the noun Adjective → describes the noun Noun → the subject of the action Adverb → modifies the verb Verb → the action Pronoun → stands in for "the fish"
Key insight: When you are learning a Spanish rule, you are usually learning how to make two of these roles play nicely together. Adjectives must match Nouns. Pronouns must replace Nouns correctly. Once you know which "job" a word is doing, every rule makes more sense.

Gender & Number Agreement
After this section you can
  • Assign masculine or feminine gender to any Spanish noun using ending patterns (-o → masc, -a → fem) and the key exceptions (Greek -ma words, abbreviations, true irregulars like la mano)

Treating all adjectives as -o/-a variable

The Greek "-ma" Rule -- Why it is masculine
Any noun ending in -ma that describes an abstract concept or academic term is almost certainly of Greek origin -- and therefore masculine: el problema, el sistema, el tema, el idioma, el programa, el clima, el poema.

These words entered Spanish through Latin from Greek, where they belonged to a neutral gender. Spanish has no neutral gender, so they all landed in masculine. The -a ending is misleading -- ignore it for these words.
The Abbreviations Rule -- la foto, la moto, la radio
La foto, la moto, and la radio are abbreviations: la foto ← la fotografia · la moto ← la motocicleta · la radio ← la radiodifusion.

When Spanish speakers shorten a word, it keeps its original gender even if the shortened version ends in -o. The gender travels with the word, not the ending.

Radio nuance: El radio (masculine) = the physical device. La radio (feminine) = the broadcast medium or station.

True exception: La mano (hand) is a genuine ancient exception that simply refused to change over thousands of years. It is not an abbreviation.
The "-cion/-sion" Rule -- Why some -ion words break the pattern
Almost every noun ending in -cion, -sion, -gion, or -xion is feminine: la cancion, la television, la region, la conexion.

The rule applies to the specific suffixes -cion, -sion, -gion, -xion. A stray -ion without C, S, G, or X in front is a different word structure -- often masculine.

Why el avion and el camion are masculine: Borrowed from French in the late 19th-20th century. In French they are masculine (un avion, un camion), and Spanish kept the original gender.

The -on augmentative suffix is aggressively masculine:
la silla → el sillon (armchair) · la puerta → el porton (large gate) · la caja → el cajon (drawer)
Any word formed with the -on augmentative suffix is automatically masculine -- even if it visually ends in the letters "ion."
One-ending adjectives — The gender-neutral team
Most beginners learn: adjectives change ending to match noun gender (rojo → roja). That rule only applies to adjectives that end in -o.

If an adjective ends in -e (like grande, interesante, inteligente) or a consonant (like fácil, popular, azul), it is a one-ending adjective. It does not have a separate masculine and feminine form. It only changes for singular vs. plural.

Presente Indicativo A1

The Present Indicative
The present indicative is your base-state tense: it describes what is happening right now, what habitually happens, and permanent or general truths. Master these endings first — every other tense is built by modifying or replacing them.
After this section you can
  • Conjugate any regular -AR, -ER, or -IR verb for all 6 pronouns in the present tense
  • Express habits, routines, and general truths using present indicative
  • Use the high-frequency irregular present forms: soy, estoy, voy, tengo, hago, quiero, puedo, sé
Conjugate regular -AR, -ER, -IR verbs in the present tense for all six pronouns.

-AR Verb · trabajar / analizar / usar
-AR · Habitual Present

This is a permanent, habitual fact about your professional life — not something happening at this exact second. "Todos los días" signals routine → Simple Present.
-ER Verb · comer

I live at The Jayne in West Adams, run through the neighborhood on Sundays, and in October I'm traveling to Minneapolis for my coffee roasting class.
Three uses of the Present Indicative in one sentence: vivo (permanent residence fact), corro (habitual weekend routine), viajo (Presente Pro-Futuro — a confirmed future plan expressed as present to signal certainty). In Mexican Spanish, using the present tense for a confirmed future event sounds more natural and certain than the future tense.
Common Errors
Error Type · Continuous for Permanent Fact
Estoy trabajando en Adobe como analista de datos. → Trabajo en Adobe como analista de datos.
The Present Continuous magnifies what is happening at this literal millisecond. Your profession is not a millisecond activity — it is a stable fact about who you are.
Error Type · Future Tense for Mexican Plans
En octubre viajaré a Minneapolis para mi clase de tueste. → En octubre viajo a Minneapolis para mi clase de tueste.
Using the morphological future ("viajaré") for a confirmed plan sounds stiff and formal in Mexican Spanish — like a news broadcast, not a conversation. The Presente Pro-Futuro ("viajo") is the natural Mexican choice because it drags the future event into the present reality, making it sound guaranteed and immediate.

Ser vs. Estar A1

Both mean "to be" — completely different functions
Ser and estar both translate as "to be" — but they cover completely different territory. Ser handles identity, origin, and permanent characteristics. Estar handles location, states, and conditions that can change. When in doubt: if it can change tomorrow, use estar.
After this section you can
  • Choose ser for identity, origin, profession, material, and characteristics that define what something is
  • Choose estar for location, health, and conditions that can change — and for progressive tenses
  • Apply the quick test: if it could be different tomorrow, use estar; if it defines the noun, use ser

Adjectives that change meaning with Ser vs. Estar — Full Context
The same adjective produces two completely different meanings depending on whether it follows ser or estar. This is not a translation error or an exception — it is the entire Ser/Estar system working exactly as designed.

The five-minute test: Ask "can this change in the next five minutes?" If yes → ESTAR (temporary state). If it describes who someone permanently is → SER (identity/character). A clever person is clever tomorrow too → SER. A ready person stops being ready once the meeting starts → ESTAR.

Why "es listo" = clever (not ready):
This is the one that confuses most English speakers. "Listo" with SER describes an innate mental quality — intelligence, sharpness, cleverness — something baked into the person's character. "Listo" with ESTAR describes a state of preparation — ready to go, prepared right now. The English word "ready" only exists as the ESTAR version. The SER version has no clean English equivalent, which is why it feels wrong.

está malo → He is sick / not feeling well (physical condition)

rico — wealth vs. taste

seguro — objective vs. subjective certainty
es seguro → It is safe / reliable (objective property of the thing itself)
está seguro → He is certain / sure (mental state of the person)

es bueno → He is a good person (moral character)

es vivo → He is sharp / cunning / street-smart (character trait)
está vivo → He is alive (current biological state)

-AR · Right This Second
No puedo contestar el teléfono — estoy hablando con un cliente de Adobe en este momento. Te llamo en diez minutos.
I can't answer the phone — I'm talking with an Adobe client right this moment. I'll call you in ten minutes.
The Present Continuous is justified here because the conversation is actively happening at the literal second the phone rings. The signal phrases "en este momento" and "ahorita" always pair correctly with the Continuous.
-ER Verb · comer
-IR Verb · escribir
-IR · Imperfect Continuous as Background
Estaba escribiendo el reporte de análisis de datos cuando mi jefe entró a la sala sin avisar y preguntó si estaba listo.
I was writing the data analysis report when my boss walked into the room without warning and asked if it was ready.
Imperfect Continuous (estaba + gerund) = the background action that was already in progress when a single completed event interrupted it. "Estaba escribiendo" is the looping background video.
Common Errors
Error Type · Continuous for Professional Identity
Estoy trabajando en Adobe como analista de datos. → Trabajo en Adobe como analista de datos.
This is the most common Continuous error for English speakers, because English uses "I'm working at Adobe" naturally for employment. In Spanish, "estoy trabajando" means you are physically performing work tasks this second — not that Adobe is your employer.
Error Type · Irregular Gerund
Estoy leendo el reporte de Snowflake. → Estoy leyendo el reporte de Snowflake.
When an -ER or -IR verb has a stem that ends in a vowel, the gerund ending -iendo becomes -yendo to avoid a triple vowel collision. Leer: le + iendo → le-iendo is awkward → leyendo.

Hay, Había, Habrá A1

Impersonal Haber — There is / There are
After this section you can
  • State that something exists (or existed) using hay — the single invariable present form of haber
  • Distinguish hay (existence — introducing something new) from está/están (location of a known thing)
  • Recognize that hay never agrees with the noun: hay un libro AND hay diez libros
State that something exists using hay, había, or habrá.
Hay · Había · Habrá — one form, three tenses, always singular
hay=present→Hay una reunión. — There is a meeting.
había=past→Había un error. — There was an error.
habrá=future→Habrá cambios. — There will be changes.
Never plural: always había / habrá regardless of how many things
The three core forms — always singular, never changes for number
hay → present: there is / there are
había → imperfect past: there was / there were (ongoing, background)
hubo → preterite: there was (completed, single event)
habrá → future: there will be
habría → conditional: there would be

Critical rule: Never use están or son for existence. Only HABER.
-AR Verb context · trabajar / haber
-AR · Hay (Present Existence)
Hay cinco reuniones en el calendario de Adobe hoy — entre ellas, una revisión de XGBoost y una llamada con el cliente internacional.
There are five meetings in the Adobe calendar today — including an XGBoost review and a call with the international client.
Hay is invariable — it never changes regardless of whether the noun that follows is singular or plural, masculine or feminine. "Hay una reunión" AND "hay cinco reuniones" both use exactly the same form.
-ER Verb context · haber / comer
-IR Verb context · haber / vivir
-IR · Hubo vs. Había Contrast
Había mucho tráfico en la 10 todos los días, y ayer hubo un accidente en la salida de Crenshaw que lo empeoró todo.
There was always a lot of traffic on the 10 every day, and yesterday there was an accident at the Crenshaw exit that made everything worse.
Both forms in one sentence to show the contrast directly. "Había mucho tráfico todos los días" — ongoing, habitual background state = Imperfect.
Common Errors
Error Type · Conjugating Hay for Number
Han muchos errores en el código de Python. → Hay muchos errores en el código de Python.
"Han" is the third-person plural of haber used in the Present Perfect ("han comido" = they have eaten). It is not the plural of hay.
Error Type · Estar for Existence
Están muchos archivos en el servidor de Adobe. → Hay muchos archivos en el servidor de Adobe.
Estar announces location of already-known, specific things. Hay announces existence — introducing something to the conversation for the first time.

The reader's prediction — built from the word's last letter
Every Spanish word ends in either a vowel/N/S or something else. That ending generates a prediction:

Word ends in… Reader predicts… Examples where prediction is CORRECT (no tilde)

Pronoun attachment — why commands cascade upward
When you attach object pronouns to a command, the word gets longer but the original stress stays on the same vowel. As the word grows, that vowel migrates further from the end — eventually requiring a tilde to protect it:

prepara → stress on PA (position 2, vowel ending → no mark needed)
prepáralo → same stress on PA, now position 3 → Esdrújula → tilde required
prepáramelo → same stress on PA, now position 4 → Sobreesdrújula → tilde required

The tilde is pinning the stress in place as the word grows. Remove it and a native speaker defaults to the new expected position — completely wrong pronunciation.
Spanish vowels — the other accent rule (always fixed)
A = "ah" · E = "eh" · I = "ee" · O = "oh" · U = "oo"
Unlike English (where "a" in "cat," "cake," "car," "about" produces four different sounds), Spanish vowels are invariant. The same vowel in the same position always sounds identical — stressed or not. This is why syllable stress is so salient in Spanish: everything else stays constant.
Accent marks as tense signals — verb forms
habló→preterite 3rd person — stress on last syllable (position 1, vowel ending) → tilde required
hablo→present 1st person — stress on position 2 (default for vowel ending) → no tilde
hablaré→future 1st person — stress on final É → tilde signals future tense
Rule: same spelling, different stress = different tense. The tilde is the only visual marker.

Error · Over-accenting default Grave words

"Datos" ends in S → default stress is position 2 → DA-tos is already correct → no tilde. "Listos" ends in S → same logic → LIS-tos → no tilde.
Error · Missing tilde on a deviation word
El examen fue facil pero util para el analisis del proyecto. → El examen fue fácil pero útil para el análisis del proyecto.
Three misses, same rule: "fácil" ends in L (consonant, not N/S) → default is stress on position 1 → but stress is on position 2 (FÁ-cil) → deviation → tilde required. "útil" same pattern: ends in L → default position 1 → stress is on position 2 (Ú-til) → tilde required.
Error · Missing verb tense accent (the most costly error)

"habló" ends in a vowel (o) → default stress is position 2. But the preterite él/ella form stresses the final syllable (ha-BLÓ, position 1) → that deviates from the default → tilde required.

Lo and La — Direct Object Pronouns ("What is being verbed?")
Lo and La replace the thing or person that directly receives the action. Lo is for masculine things/people ("him" or "it for masculine"). La is for feminine ("her" or "it for feminine").

Tengo el pasaporte. → Lo tengo. I have it. (el pasaporte → lo)
Veo a María. → La veo. I see her. (María → la)
Leo la receta. → La leo. I read it. (la receta → la)
Le — Indirect Object Pronoun ("To whom / for whom?")
Le replaces the person who receives something or for whom an action is done. It means "to him," "to her," or "to you (formal)." Critically: le is gender-neutral — it is the same for a man or a woman.

Le doy el libro a Juan. I give the book to him / to Juan.
Le digo la verdad a María. I tell the truth to her / to María.
Le escribo un correo. I write an email to him/her/you.

The cheat code for lo/la vs. le: Is there a physical object or piece of information being transferred? If the verb is about giving, telling, sending, showing, or buying something for someone → you need le for the person receiving the thing.

When both appear together: imagine buying a coffee (el café) for a friend (Juan).
What is bought? The coffee (el café) = direct object → lo.
Who receives it? Juan = indirect object → le.
Side by side: le lo sounds clunky, so grammar changes le → se: se lo compro.
Pronoun placement — Two valid positions with one verb phrase
When you have a conjugated verb + infinitive (like vas a tomar), you have two valid placements for the pronoun. Both are correct — choose based on flow.

1. Attached to the infinitive (most common in spoken Spanish):
Vas a tomarlo. — You are going to drink it.

2. Before the conjugated verb:
Lo vas a tomar. — You are going to drink it.

The rule: the pronoun must be attached to or immediately before the verbal unit. It can never float in the middle.
The Optical Illusion — Why lo/la do NOT match the verb ending
Looking at lo tomo and la toma, it is tempting to think the "o" and "a" must match — that lo goes with a verb ending in -o. Throw that idea out. Verbs do not have gender. The pronoun and the verb are doing two completely separate jobs.

The golden rule:
→ The pronoun (lo/la) tells you the gender of the thing being verbed.
→ The verb ending (-o, -as, -a) tells you who is doing it.

Sentence Pronoun means… Verb ending means…
Lo tomo. lo = the thing is masculine (el espresso) -o on tomo = I do it (yo)
La toma. la = the thing is feminine (la leche) -a on toma = he/she/you(formal) does it

The -o at the end of tomo is simply the "I" form of the verb — it has nothing to do with masculine or feminine. A woman says lo tomo and a man says lo tomo. The verb does not care about the gender of the speaker.

-AR · Además — Adding Force

Remote work saves commute time. Furthermore, working from home improves concentration for data analysis in Snowflake.
"Además" adds a second argument that reinforces and strengthens the first — it is an additive connector. Punctuation rule: Además always starts the new clause (after a period or semicolon) and is followed by a comma: "Además, [clause]." Never mid-sentence without punctuation.
-ER Verb · comer / hacer
-IR Verb · vivir / salir
-IR · Por Eso + Porque — Cause and Effect Chain
Vivo en The Jayne en West Adams porque el barrio tiene mucha personalidad — por eso salgo a correr por sus calles cada domingo.
I live at The Jayne in West Adams because the neighborhood has a lot of character — that's why I go out running through its streets every Sunday.
"Porque" introduces the cause (the neighborhood's character motivated the choice to live there). "Por eso" introduces the consequence (the love of the neighborhood leads to the Sunday runs).
Common Errors
Error Type · Sin Embargo Without Punctuation Boundaries
Me gusta Adobe sin embargo el trayecto es muy largo todos los días. → Me gusta Adobe. Sin embargo, el trayecto es muy largo todos los días.
"Sin embargo" is a sentence connector — it must be preceded by a period (or semicolon) and followed by a comma. Using it mid-sentence without any punctuation creates a run-on that is penalized on the B1 writing exam.
Error Type · Porque/Por Eso Directional Confusion
No terminé el análisis de Snowflake porque eso tuve reuniones todo el día. → Tuve reuniones todo el día; por eso no terminé el análisis de Snowflake.
"Porque eso" fuses two separate connectors into a nonsensical hybrid. "Porque" (one word) introduces the cause and looks backward.

Mexican Spanish Notes B1

Regional patterns, shortcuts, and cultural phrases from Mexican street-level speech
After this section you can
  • Swap estar for andar in casual speech to express being actively caught up in doing something: ando buscando
  • Default to the preterite for all past events — including recent ones — instead of the present perfect
  • Deploy Que + present subjunctive as a polished, natural sign-off in social and professional settings
Standard Spanish is the foundation — but in Mexico, the language has regional shortcuts, tense preferences, and social formulas that differ from what textbooks teach. These are the patterns you will actually hear.
Recognize and use key Mexican Spanish variants in casual and professional contexts.
1. The "Andar" Swap — the Mexican estar for continuous action
In standard Spanish, ongoing actions use estar + gerundio: Estoy trabajando (I am working).

In Mexico — especially casual or young speech — people swap estar for andar (to walk/move around) to express being actively in the middle of doing something. It carries a "going around doing X" or "being caught up in X" vibe.

Standard Mexican
Estoy buscando un hotel. Ando buscando un hotel.
¿Qué estás haciendo? ¿Qué andas haciendo?
2. Simple Past Wins Everything — no present perfect in casual Mexican Spanish
Spanish has two ways to talk about the recent past. In Spain, recent events use the Present Perfect: Hoy he ido a la tienda (Today I have gone to the store).

Mexican Spanish almost completely ignores this construction in casual speech. The sharp, simple Preterite handles everything — including events that happened five minutes ago.
Mexican preference
Fui a la tienda. · Ya comí. · ¿Qué dijiste?
I went to the store. · I already ate. · What did you say?
3. The "Que" Formula — institutional well-wishes
In hotels, restaurants, airports, and social events, you will hear a barrage of phrases starting with Que followed by a subjunctive verb. It is a shortened version of "Espero que [verb]" (I hope that...) — an efficient polite sign-off.

Que te vaya bien. May things go well for you. (The gold standard goodbye.)
Que tengan buen vuelo. May you (plural) have a good flight.
Que se diviertan. May you (plural) have fun. (To friends heading out.)
Que disfrutes. May you enjoy it. (Barista or server handing you a drink or meal.)

Structure: Que + presente subjuntivo. The verb is subjunctive because you are expressing a wish, not stating a fact.
